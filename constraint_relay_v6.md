# The AI Constraint Relay: How the Compute Build-Out Displaces Physical and Financial Limits

## 1. Scope and method

Most AI-infrastructure commentary argues over demand, that is, whether software revenue can justify the capital being spent. This assessment takes the supply side instead: the physical, material, and geographic limits on building and running the compute, and the mechanism through which those limits displace across power, labour, equipment, and capital.

**Scope.** The register tracks six factors across the infrastructure stack. Four are physical and material limits on building and running the compute: ① capital durability and underwriting capacity, ② power, labour, and physical delivery, ③ leading-edge fabrication and single-source materials, and ④ manufactured and licensed chokepoints. Two act on those limits:

- **⑤ Commoditisation from below** is assessed strictly through capital durability. The question here is not whether demand for tokens falls. It is what happens to the yield on a deployed megawatt when open weights push token prices toward marginal energy cost: the revenue servicing the assets in ① degrades while the assets themselves, and their refresh clock, do not. That is a write-down channel, which is the lens ① already uses.
- **⑥ Export controls** are treated as a constraint on availability rather than on cost. In June 2026 a Bureau of Industry and Security instrument took two frontier models off the market worldwide for a period of days. A force that removes supply belongs in the register; a force that prices it is an amplifier, and the amplifiers are in §5.

**Horizon.** This assessment is written for the twelve to twenty-four months from August 2026, which is the period over which the checkpoints in §4 fall and over which the write-down clock in ① runs. The non-relocatable tails in ③ and the licensed leg of ④ extend well beyond it; where a constraint runs longer than the horizon, that is stated at the constraint.

**On method, the honest split.** The constraints in §2 are verifiable, sourced, dated numbers, and they carry the argument. The constraint relay reading in §3 is *interpretation of* those numbers and is labelled as such. No likelihood or severity scores are asserted: assigning 1 to 5 ratings would dress judgement as measurement. Every figure is dated, and a companion `reproduce.py` tabulates and cites each one from `supply_constraints.csv`.

**On verification.** For this edition the event-driven and structural claims were checked against their issuing sources (the US Energy Information Administration, the International Energy Agency, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, PJM, the US Bureau of Reclamation, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, the US Geological Survey, Japan METI, the European Commission, company filings, and regulatory dockets). Market-live readings are 14 August 2026 market-close readings (with 21 July 2026 comparators noted) and will move. Where two sources give different values for the same quantity, both are shown rather than averaged.

## 2. The six factors (the data)

**① Capital spend running ahead of revenue, and the credit channel underneath it.** Combined 2026 capital guidance across the big-four hyperscalers sums to approximately $720 to $745B following the late-July 2026 Q2 prints (Alphabet $195 to $205B, raised on 22 July 2026 from $180 to $190B; Amazon guided full-year cash capex to approximately $220B on 30 July 2026; Meta $130 to $145B, narrowed on 29 July 2026 from $125 to $145B; Microsoft guided stated cash capex to $175B on 29 July 2026, which is net of the reclassification described below and is therefore not on the same accounting boundary as the other three). Across the top five, guidance sums to approximately $760 to $780B, while reported aggregates track at $670 to $710B, so the number is better read as a range than as a point. Accelerators carry a roughly 2 to 3-year refresh clock, so unmonetised clusters face write-downs on a 12 to 36-month horizon.

**Two qualifications belong here, and both cut against the starkest reading of this factor.**

First, part of the increase is price rather than capacity. Amazon raised its 2026 capex guidance by $20B and explicitly attributed the raise to hardware cost inflation. Chief Executive Andy Jassy stated on 30 July 2026: "The higher cost of memory is pushing this number up from our prior estimate of about $200 billion." Microsoft similarly attributes roughly $25B of its capital plan to higher component pricing, and Meta's raise cites memory pricing alongside datacentre facility costs. A guidance raise driven by the chokepoint in ④ is cost inflation passing through the capex line, not additional conviction about demand, and the two should not be read as the same signal.

Second, the revenue side of the comparison is the weakest number in this register. Incremental annual AI revenue has been carried at $20 to $30B, an analyst estimate, and 2026 company disclosures suggest that understates it: Microsoft reported an AI business at a $37B annualised run rate, up 123 per cent year on year, and Google Cloud grew 63 per cent year on year to $20.02B in Q1 2026 (and 82 per cent in Q2), with Alphabet describing itself as compute constrained in the near term. Those are company-reported figures defined differently from an industry increment, so they do not simply replace it, but the gap between capital and revenue is probably narrower than a $720B against $20 to $30B framing implies. The comparison remains directional rather than a precise ratio: total hyperscaler capex, not all of it AI-specific, set against an AI-only revenue increment.

Four additions sharpen this factor in 2026. 

First, scale off balance sheet: Moody's reports approximately $662B of datacentre lease commitments signed but not commenced, sitting off balance sheet under GAAP and larger than the same firms' combined on-balance-sheet debt. 

Second, accounting useful-life extensions distort headline capex comparisons: Microsoft extended the estimated useful life of **datacentres and office buildings** from 15 to 25 years effective at the start of FY2027, and will book more future datacentre leases as operating rather than finance leases. Server and network equipment lives are unchanged and are disclosed in the FY2026 Form 10-K at two to six years; v5 stated this extension applied to servers, which was wrong. The CFO stated the change has a "minimal benefit to FY27 operating income", that "the greater impact is on capital expenditures", that "finance leases are included in capital expenditures while operating leases are not", and that "outside of this useful life impact, our calendar year 2026 CapEx investment expectations remain unchanged". The stated $175B is therefore an accounting boundary change, not a reduction in physical commitment, and any cross-company capex comparison that does not add operating lease commitments back will understate Microsoft against its peers.

Third, the debt itself has been quantified: JPMorgan projects roughly $4.1T of AI-related debt financing through 2030, within an estimated $5.5T of total AI capital spending, and notes that AI-related debt already accounts for about 15 per cent of the corporate-bond universe, with more than $300B issued in 2026 to date. 

Fourth, this debt is unusually rate-sensitive: it is long-duration paper against long-payback infrastructure, so the Warsh Fed's higher-for-longer stance (held at 3.50 to 3.75 per cent for a fifth consecutive meeting on 29 July 2026, with a median end-2026 projection of 3.8 per cent from the June 2026 Summary of Economic Projections) raises the carrying cost faster than a token-price decline would. Rates remain the macro lever most likely to turn the build-out's leverage toxic, ahead of any demand disappointment.

**Market prices through the mid-2026 cycle have inverted the neat financing gradient proposed in earlier editions.** Previous drafts suggested a clean sorting from self-funded balance sheets (Meta holding up) through cyclical producers (Micron) to debt-financed counterparty exposures (Oracle collapsing). That reading has not held on the tape. 

Between the 21 July 2026 close and the 14 August 2026 close (Nasdaq historical data), Meta fell 8.4 per cent from $643.81 to $589.85 as investors digested its raised capex guidance and multi-billion-dollar compute commitments. Over the same window, Oracle rebounded 18.5 per cent from $127.05 (and off its 9 July BBB- rating trough of ≈$121) to $150.52, as markets treated its $638B contracted backlog (approximately half with OpenAI) as durable forward revenue rather than immediate default risk. Micron traded virtually flat across that window, moving from $970.82 to $971.66, having already corrected 22.6 per cent from its 25 June all-time intraday high of $1,255.50. Equity markets appear to penalise hyperscaler cash absorption regardless of balance sheet strength, while treating single-counterparty risk as a secondary consideration while contract backlogs grow.

**Oracle remains an important indicator of structural financing mechanics.** The financing in this build-out is substantially circular: labs buy cloud capacity from hyperscalers, hyperscalers and chip vendors hold equity in the labs, chip vendors invest in the deployers who buy their chips, and hyperscalers prepay memory makers years ahead of delivery. Concentration risk inside a structure like that is not confined to the counterparty where it currently happens to be visible. A single renegotiation propagates through the same debt stack rather than stopping at one balance sheet, which is what makes the $4.1T figure above a systemic number rather than a sum of independent exposures.

**Insurance and reinsurance capacity limits set a physical ceiling on syndicated debt.** Multi-gigawatt campus clusters represent $15B to $40B+ in Total Insured Value at a single geographic point. Commercial property and reinsurance underwriters (Lloyd's syndicates, Munich Re, Swiss Re) enforce treaty accumulation caps on single-site catastrophe and electrical fire exposure. Where insurance markets refuse to absorb additional concentration, debt syndication and project-financing covenants stall physically regardless of borrower credit rating.

The reallocation has landed in reported earnings. IBM fell 25.21 per cent on 14 July 2026, its worst single day on record, erasing about $68.8B, after pre-announcing a quarter in which clients pulled memory and hardware spend forward and funded it by deferring software, mainframe, and consulting outlays. That is a crowd-out channel: capital going into the chokepoint described in ④ is partly coming out of adjacent IT budgets rather than out of new money.

Two deflations belong with this factor. Investment-grade credit spreads were 80 bps on 14 August 2026 (ICE BofA US Corporate Index OAS via FRED, series BAMLC0A0CM, up modestly from 74 bps in June 2026), and high-yield spreads were 2.82 per cent (FRED series BAMLH0A0HYM2, up from 2.75 per cent in June 2026). Stress remains issuer-specific rather than a broad market widening.

Finally, the guidance-cut trigger has failed to fire across four consecutive quarters (all big-four spenders raised or held guidance in late July 2026), even as free cash flow contracted (Amazon trailing-twelve-month free cash flow fell to approximately $1.2B, down about 95 per cent year on year). The informative signal now shifts to the gap between hyperscaler capex and merchant accelerator revenues: internal custom silicon (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium) and AMD merchant options allow capex to rise while Nvidia's take-rate softens.

**② The binding constraint has moved from chips to the grid, and the price signal is now capped.** Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects US datacentre demand rising from 176 TWh in 2023 (approximately 4.4 per cent of US electricity) to 325 to 580 TWh (6.7 to 12 per cent) by 2028. The IEA's data annex puts global datacentre electricity at approximately 416 TWh in 2024, reaching approximately 946 TWh by 2030 in its Base Case, within a scenario range of 669 TWh (Headwinds) to 1,264 TWh (Lift-Off); fleet power-usage effectiveness improves over the period (1.41 in 2024 to approximately 1.29 projected). An AI compute rack draws 30 to 100+ kW against 7 to 10 kW for a conventional rack.

Across PJM's 2026/27 and 2027/28 capacity auctions, datacentre load accounted for approximately $13.8B of capacity cost ($7.3B then $6.5B; PJM's independent market monitor, Monitoring Analytics), and 28 to 31 per cent of projects drop out at the first interconnection decision even under fast-track rules. The 2026/27 auction cleared at $329.17/MW-day against $28.92 for 2024/25.

**The 2028/29 auction (cleared 14 July 2026) marked a structural framing shift.** It settled at the $325/MW-day cap, about 2.5 per cent below the prior cap, held there by a price collar agreed with the region's states and FERC; PJM estimates that without the collar it would have cleared at $554.72 and cost about $29.7B rather than $16.4B. It cleared 6,831 MW short, with datacentres named the primary demand driver and only 525 MW of new generation clearing. 

Post-auction regulatory filings have transformed this volume deficit into an active administrative rationing regime. On 31 July 2026 PJM filed a proposal with FERC for a $20B dedicated generation procurement program serving datacentres through 2027. On 7 August 2026 PJM submitted a bring-your-own-power framework establishing formal service limits and mandatory on-site generation rules for loads of 50 MW or greater post-2027.

**Skilled electrical labour is the non-tradable bottleneck underneath the build-out.** Substation construction, transmission tie-ins, and switchyard commissioning require certified high-voltage electricians (US BLS SOC 47-2111) and power-line installers (SOC 49-9051). Unlike manufactured equipment, skilled journeymen cannot be imported or substituted with capital: certified electrical apprenticeships require four to five years of structured training (US Department of Labor Registered Apprenticeship data). Trade wage growth outstripping the general Employment Cost Index and utility project delays attributed to crew availability indicate that labour sets an inelastic temporal floor on energisation.

**Prime movers and turbine castings close the on-site generation bypass.** To escape the 3 to 5-year grid queue, developers turn to behind-the-meter generation, but prime mover manufacturing has its own multi-year queue. Industrial gas turbine production slots (heavy-duty F-class and H-class) at GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Power are backlogged for three to five years in SEC Form 10-Q filings. Underneath turbine assembly sits a concentrated casting chokepoint: vacuum-induction investment casting of single-crystal superalloy turbine blades (Howmet Aerospace, Precision Castparts) is shared with backlogged commercial aerospace engine programmes, preventing rapid capacity scaling.

**Grid equipment beyond transformers faces matching lead times.** While Large Power Transformers run 128-week lead times, Generator Step-Up units 144 weeks, and Extra-High-Voltage units 36 to 48 months (Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025 survey, corroborated by the US Department of Energy), adjacent transmission hardware is equally constrained. High-voltage gas-insulated switchgear, SF6-free circuit breakers, and high-voltage direct-current cables run three to five-year delivery lead times (Hitachi Energy, Prysmian, NKT).

**Water constraints remove sites through administrative moratoria.** Beyond cooling expenditure, water availability acts as a physical siting filter. US Bureau of Reclamation 24-Month Studies for the Colorado River Basin establish shortage guidelines that govern regional allocations, while state groundwater authorities (Arizona Department of Water Resources Active Management Areas) and municipal councils in water-stressed basins enforce outright permit moratoria on high-consumption facilities, eliminating candidate sites from the development pipeline.

**Nuclear baseload and SMR deployment are bounded by licensing and fuel enrichment.** Hyperscaler pivots toward nuclear PPAs and Small Modular Reactors face a three to five-year Nuclear Regulatory Commission licensing review cycle for plant restarts and design approvals. SMR architectures requiring High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU, 5 to 20 per cent U-235) face a fuel chokepoint: Western commercial enrichment capacity is in pilot stages (Centrus Energy), while historical supply was concentrated in Russia.

**One qualification on using PJM as the gauge.** PJM is a single US regional transmission organisation with its own price collar and state politics, so it is a partial instrument rather than a national one. It is also, increasingly, measuring only the load that stayed in the queue: behind-the-meter generation, direct power-purchase agreements, and off-grid energy islands take deployment outside the clearing mechanism altogether. The shortfall should therefore be read as a floor under the constraint rather than as its full national extent.

**Energy price shocks feed back into ① as a baseline cost condition rather than a tail risk.** A mid-July 2026 stacking of a Strait of Hormuz re-escalation and Russia's full diesel-export ban pushed Brent to approximately $88. As of 11 August 2026, the EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook expects Brent crude spot prices to average $86.81 per barrel across full-year 2026 ($85 in Q3, $78 in Q4), with price relief ($69 in 2027) delayed until early 2027 as shut-in Middle East output and Hormuz transit constraints ease slowly. The prior assumption that prices would return toward $70 by Q4 2026 on OPEC+ spare capacity has been superseded. Elevated energy input costs are now the central case for full-year 2026 operating expenditure.

Two corrections attach here. Per-query energy has been revised downward: the widely-cited approximately 2.9 Wh figure has given way to a median Gemini text prompt at approximately 0.24 Wh (Google) and approximately 0.34 Wh (OpenAI) for 2025, though heavier reasoning queries run at approximately 18 to 40 Wh. And the projection literature is wide: the IEA 4E EDNA review documents 2030 datacentre energy projections spanning 200 TWh to 8,000 TWh across 100+ studies (a 40-fold range), triangulates 2023 at 300 to 380 TWh, and attributes the widest figures to crude temporal extrapolation. Lake Powell minimum power pool breach (3,490 ft) remains deferred past 2026: the US Bureau of Reclamation's July 2026 Probable Minimum trace projects 3,503.65 ft at 31 December 2026 (approximately 13.7 ft above the 3,490 ft power pool, with unregulated inflows at 36 per cent of average and annual releases reduced to 6.00 million acre-feet, while Lake Mead sits at 1,037.10 ft). The Reclamation traces carry a vintage mismatch across scenarios (Current Minimum Probable is July 2026, Current Maximum Probable is May 2026, and the canonical August file remains the August 2025 release), but the power pool deferral holds across every available trace.

**③ Leading-edge fabrication is a single geography, and its material foundation is concentrated.** TSMC produces approximately 90 per cent of leading-edge logic, and CoWoS advanced packaging is effectively all Taiwan-based with no second source. Diversification leaves leading-edge production Taiwan-bound through roughly 2028 to 2030. The capability cannot be seized by force: lithography tools are reportedly capable of remote disablement and depend on imported inputs, so a Strait disruption would mean a global compute freeze rather than a transfer of capacity. This factor remains the non-relocatable tail event, striking chips, shipping lanes, and the financial system at once.

Three physical and chemical nodes deepen this geographic concentration tail:

*Substrates (ABF).* High-density interconnect packaging for large-die logic depends on Ajinomoto Build-up Film substrates. Manufacturing is heavily concentrated in Japan and Taiwan (Ibiden, Shinko Electric, Unimicron), where substrate yields decline non-linearly as package surface area expands.

*Specialty EUV photoresists and precursors.* More than 85 per cent of advanced EUV and immersion photoresists, photoacid generators, and fluorinated polymers are manufactured by Japanese chemical specialists (Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, JSR Corporation, Shin-Etsu Chemical). These materials remain governed by Japan METI export control frameworks, creating a sole-region chemical chokepoint upstream of all leading-edge wafer processing.

*High-purity quartz (HPQ) crucibles.* Single-crystal silicon ingot pulling requires ultra-high-purity quartz crucibles capable of withstanding 1,450 degrees Celsius. Two producers operate around Spruce Pine, North Carolina (Sibelco, The Quartz Corp), and their material exceeds 99.999 per cent purity, which is the highest published grade. **The world-share figure carried through v5 is withdrawn at v6.** Versions v4 and v5 stated that 80 to 90 per cent of world semiconductor-grade quartz feedstock originates at Spruce Pine, cited to USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries. The MCS 2026 quartz chapter states "World Mine Production and Reserves: This information was not available", and records that import statistics for lascas and HPQ cannot be separated from other quartz types. USGS publishes no world share, so none is asserted here. The same chapter records that on 10 April China's Ministry of Natural Resources announced over 35 million tons of HPQ reserves in Henan and Xinjiang at 99.995 to 99.998 per cent purity. Crucible specification is set by trace-element limits rather than headline purity, so a grade advantage is not by itself a volume chokepoint.

**④ Manufactured and licensed single-points-of-failure, and the chokepoint set has widened.** Beyond logic, the chain narrows to a few suppliers.

*Memory, and price incidence inversion.* An HBM and DRAM shortage runs to approximately 2028, with Micron and SK Hynix sold out through 2027 to 2028 and SK Hynix leadership projecting tightness into 2030. However, the price incidence runs opposite to how standard commentary describes it. HBM takes approximately 22 per cent of DRAM wafer input in 2026 (18 per cent in 2025, 30 per cent in 2027) while supplying only about 9 per cent of DRAM bits, and that wafer-to-bit gap is the crowding-out mechanism. **The margin incentive behind it has inverted, and v5 did not record this.** TrendForce's June 2026 per-wafer analysis finds HBM wafer revenue was overtaken by DDR5 64GB RDIMM in Q1 2026, with HBM profitability below DDR5 64GB RDIMM since. v5 stated that HBM earns three to five times higher revenue per wafer; on the same source family that is no longer the case. 

As capacity reallocates toward HBM, trade-press consensus estimates (TrendForce, DigiTimes, Counterpoint Research) indicate that full-year 2026 HBM contract average selling prices (ASPs) have risen moderately (+1 per cent at SK Hynix, +8 per cent at Samsung, +22 per cent at Micron). The severe price spikes have landed on the shrunken commodity DRAM pool left behind: traditional DRAM revenue per bit surged +116 per cent at Samsung, +78 per cent at SK Hynix, and +54 per cent at Micron. The AI buyer is not paying the primary inflation; handset makers, PC OEMs, and consumer device buyers are. This chokepoint represents a cost transfer across buyer classes rather than a direct barrier to AI compute deployment.

*Automated Test Equipment (ATE) scaling.* HBM3e and HBM4 architectures require four to six times the test duration of commodity DRAM across wafer-level probe and final stack verification. Test slot capacity at Advantest and Teradyne operates as an inelastic physical throughput governor on delivered memory volumes.

*Optical transceivers and InP lasers.* Scaling compute clusters to 100,000+ accelerators requires high-speed optical transceivers (800G and 1.6T) powered by Continuous-Wave Indium Phosphide laser diodes for silicon photonics and co-packaged optics. Production is concentrated across specialized cleanrooms at Coherent, Lumentum, and Broadcom.

*Coupled financing nodes.* Frontier AI labs and the memory chokepoint are now linked by balance sheet equity. Anthropic's Series H round ($65B, $965B post-money, closed 28 May 2026) brought in Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix as strategic infrastructure partners alongside $5B from Amazon. The three producers that constitute the memory chokepoint now hold equity stakes in frontier model demand. Factors ① and ④ are no longer independent; a demand shock and a supply shock share a single financing node.

*Packaging and optics.* CoWoS remains the primary bottleneck. ASE Technology raised 2026 capex to $8.5B as the critical node for co-packaged optics, representing a second distinct packaging chokepoint. EUV lithography optics remain sole-sourced through ASML and Zeiss.

*Materials, licensed.* China refines most of the world's rare earths and dominates heavy-rare-earth separation and magnet-making; earlier editions of this paper gave approximately 91 per cent of refining and approximately 60 per cent of magnet-grade mining (IEA, with Rhodium Group). Western magnet alternatives (MP Materials, USA Rare Earth) are not expected at full production before 2027. Beijing's April 2025 per-shipment licensing over seven medium and heavy rare earths pushed EU heavy-REE prices to roughly six times domestic levels. The October 2025 extraterritorial 0.1 per cent rule remains suspended under a US-China truce scheduled for formal review around November 2026.

*Materials, physical.* The 28 February 2026 strike on Qatar's Ras Laffan removed an estimated 30 per cent of semiconductor-grade helium. TSMC reported no notable impact due to inventory buffers, with the disruption surfacing in helium-sealed enterprise storage drives rather than logic output. Physical buffered inputs represent months-long shocks; licensed structural monopolies represent multi-year constraints.

**⑤ Commoditisation from below.** Open models compress the frontier premium. Moonshot's Kimi K3 (16 July 2026) is the current datapoint: a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model priced at $3/$15 per million tokens, scoring near the top closed tier on aggregate benchmarks. A price index of frontier tokens fell approximately 20 per cent from its May 2026 peak as usage substituted toward cheaper options. Chinese industrial policy is explicitly aligned behind open weights, with Xi framing open-weight architectures as a global public good at the July 2026 World AI Conference.

**A caveat on how this factor is measured.** The lens stated in §1 is yield on a deployed megawatt, and that quantity is not directly observable: firms do not report revenue per megawatt of deployed capacity, and a token price index is a directional proxy rather than a direct measurement. What can be observed is the direction of the two terms separately, with token prices falling while capital cost per megawatt rises on component pricing. This factor is carried on a directional argument rather than on a measured ratio.

> **Inverse-risk row: the over-capacity tail and the Micron paradox.**
>
> **The claim.** Hyperscalers leasing surplus compute (xAI leasing Colossus 1 to Anthropic and Google; Meta Compute developing capacity rental offerings) is read by some analysts as early evidence of overbuild, which would flatten the HBM demand curve.
>
> **The co-existence of shortage and glut.** In August 2026, the shortage tail and the glut tail are visible inside the same corporate disclosures. Micron is sold out of DRAM and HBM through 2027, yet its equity closed at $971.66 on 14 August 2026, down 22.6 per cent from its 25 June intraday peak ($1,255.50). Samsung traded down 33 per cent from its peak, and SK Hynix fell 15 per cent, breaking its Nasdaq ADR issue price. Against sold-out datacentre order books, IDC projects 2026 global smartphone shipments to contract by 14 per cent (the largest annual drop on record), prompting Micron to exit consumer memory entirely. The order books reflect physical datacentre shortage; the equity valuations reflect end-consumer demand destruction caused by component cost pass-through.
>
> **Why this register retains the constraint relay.** Leasing spare capacity reflects ordinary cloud capital efficiency and burn mitigation. Demand strength from compute-constrained labs (Anthropic) drove the lease proposals, while memory order books sit locked on 3 to 5-year contracts with prepayments up to 30 per cent. Meta's CoreWeave contract reportedly contains a no-sublease provision barring resale of capacity it rents, and surplus capacity can also be inferred as evidence of a large, growing market rather than a glut. The inverse tail is monitored rather than adopted as the base case.
>
> **Upgrade trigger (none tripped as of 15 August 2026):** multiple hyperscalers turning net sellers; published idle-fleet utilisation confirming sustained low use; HBM contracts renegotiated downward; or outright capex guidance cuts.

**⑥ The export-control two-stack, now reaching model access.** On 12 June 2026 the Commerce Department used an "Is-Informed" letter to require export licences for foreign access to Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5. The models went dark worldwide before trusted-partner carve-outs were established on 26 June and restored in July. OpenAI limited the release of GPT-5.6 at administrative request days later. As President Macron warned at the G7, arbitrary access suspension spurs sovereign, locally-hosted model procurement across the EU, Gulf, and Japan, subsidising open-weight alternatives.

**One limit on this instrument.** Model-access control via hosted endpoints is soft enforcement set against a physical chokepoint. Weights leak, API traffic can be proxied through neutral jurisdictions, and local fine-tuning sits outside hosted endpoints entirely. The June episode bit because Fable and Mythos were closed-weight hosted models; it cannot reach downloadable open weights.

Hardware controls continue. H200 sales approved in December 2025 under a 25 per cent tax stalled by March 2026 under dual-end security screening and Chinese domestic substitution rules. Global governance is bifurcating along the same axis: the China-led World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO, 29 founding states) launched in July 2026 against the US-led Pax Silica alliance (35 member states). EU AI Act GPAI enforcement took effect on 2 August 2026 (fines up to 3 per cent of global turnover), while Annex III high-risk compliance remains gated on harmonised CEN-CENELEC standards, delaying full implementation by up to sixteen months.

## 3. The constraint relay: hydraulic displacement and moving ceilings

The evidence compiled across v1 through v6 refutes the static assumption of a bimodal distribution with a thin middle. In the live register, 12 of 14 triggers sit in active intermediate states ("Elevated", "Backlogged", "Concentrated", "Suspended under truce"). The system is not bifurcating into either immediate collapse or frictionless growth; it is operating under continuous hydraulic displacement.

**The displacement mechanism.** Every time a constraint threatens to bind, capital and policy re-route the bottleneck toward the nearest un-hedged node or the party with the least pricing power:

1. *From chips to the grid:* As advanced logic availability scaled, the constraint shifted to utility grid interconnections and substation capacity.
2. *From price to volume:* When regional power auction prices spiked ($329.17/MW-day), regulatory collars capped the price signal at $325/MW-day, converting the constraint into a 6,831 MW physical volume deficit.
3. *From volume to administrative rationing:* Volume deficits triggered formal regulatory rationing regimes (PJM's $20B procurement and 50 MW bring-your-own-power mandates).
4. *From rationing to on-site generation queues:* Developers pivoted to behind-the-meter turbines to bypass the grid queue, immediately hitting 3 to 5-year OEM manufacturing backlogs (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi).
5. *From prime movers to casting capacity and labour:* Turbine assembly backlogs pushed pressure into single-crystal superalloy blade foundries (Howmet, Precision Castparts), while substation energisation hit an inelastic human ceiling: certified high-voltage electricians and linemen requiring four to five-year apprenticeships that cannot be imported.
6. *Incidence inversion, with its stated motive now in doubt:* In memory, wafer reallocation toward HBM shrank conventional DRAM supply and transferred cost inflation to consumer device makers. The allocation effect is well evidenced. The margin motive asserted for it is not: since Q1 2026 HBM per-wafer revenue has sat below DDR5 64GB RDIMM. Either suppliers reallocate back and the incidence unwinds, or allocation is held for contract and qualification reasons, in which case the margin explanation was never the driver. This is a live falsifier, not a settled step.

**The non-relocatable tail.** While base-case constraints continuously relocate through adjacent supply chains, the tail risks in ③ (Taiwan Strait blockade, Japanese chemical licensing) and ④ (licensed rare earth embargos, semiconductor-grade helium) represent non-relocatable boundaries: physical single-points-of-failure where displacement is impossible and capacity cannot be re-routed. **Membership changed at v6 in both directions.** Spruce Pine quartz leaves the set, because the world share on which its classification rested is not published by the source it was attributed to. Helium enters it: v4 and v5 classified the Ras Laffan outage as an inventory-buffered problem measured in months, on the correct observation that fabs held three to six months of cover and leading-edge output was not disrupted. The buffer was real and the classification was wrong. Company officials indicate repairs of three to five years, and a six-month buffer against a multi-year outage delays the point at which a constraint binds without providing anywhere for it to relocate to. Semiconductor manufacturing consumed an estimated 24 per cent of global helium in 2025 and there is no substitute input.

## 5. Cost amplifiers (policy, not standalone factors)

**US tariffs** raise the landed cost of hardware and grid equipment. The average effective US tariff rate was approximately 9.1 per cent in 2026, the highest since 1946. Following the Supreme Court's 20 February 2026 ruling (*Learning Resources v. Trump*) invalidating IEEPA tariff authority, duties were reinstated same-day under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. Tariffs amplify capital friction without altering underlying physical limits.

**Refrigerant and F-gas regulations** raise cooling system capital and maintenance expenditure. Under the US AIM Act and EU F-Gas III regulations, phasedown quotas on hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) increase chiller hardware costs and synthetic fluid reclamation expenses, pricing thermal management rather than physically preventing it.

**Ratepayer tariff design** shifts grid reinforcement costs directly to large-load users. State public utility commissions increasingly authorise large-load tariff riders, accelerated infrastructure surcharges, and minimum take-or-pay contract structures. These mechanisms price electricity access without eliminating physical grid capacity (unless converted into an outright municipal building moratorium).

**Land and dark-fibre routing premiums** increase real estate acquisition and transport network build-out costs in primary datacentre clusters (Northern Virginia, Silicon Valley), redirecting capital outlays toward secondary regions without halting overall deployment.
