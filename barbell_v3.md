# The AI Capital-Infrastructure Barbell: A Supply-Side Risk Assessment

*Version 3 · NM AI Research (ORCID 0009-0003-4213-7769) · CC BY 4.0 · July 2026 · concept DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20586863 · supersedes v2.2 (10.5281/zenodo.21191963), v2.1 (10.5281/zenodo.20708803), v2 (10.5281/zenodo.20707214) and v1 (10.5281/zenodo.20586864)*

## 1. Scope and method

Most AI-infrastructure commentary argues over demand, that is, whether software revenue can justify the capital being spent. This assessment takes the supply side instead: the physical, material, and geographic limits on building and running the compute, and the shape of risk they create.

**Scope.** The register carries six factors. Four are limits on building and running the compute: ① capital durability, ② power, ③ leading-edge fabrication, and ④ the manufactured and licensed chokepoints in between. Two act on those limits rather than sitting beside them, and both are assessed through the same lens as the other four rather than as demand judgements:

- **⑤ Commoditisation from below** is assessed strictly through capital durability. The question here is not whether demand for tokens falls. It is what happens to the yield on a deployed megawatt when open weights push token prices toward marginal energy cost: the revenue servicing the assets in ① degrades while the assets themselves, and their refresh clock, do not. That is a write-down channel, which is the lens ① already uses.
- **⑥ Export controls** are treated as a constraint on availability rather than on cost. In June 2026 a Bureau of Industry and Security instrument took two frontier models off the market worldwide for a period of days. A force that removes supply belongs in the register; a force that prices it is an amplifier, and the amplifiers are in §5.

**Horizon.** This assessment is written for the twelve to twenty-four months from July 2026, which is the period over which the check-points in §4 fall and over which the write-down clock in ① runs. The tails in ③ and the licensed leg of ④ extend well beyond it; where a constraint runs longer than the horizon, that is stated at the constraint.

**On method, the honest split.** The constraints in §2 are verifiable, sourced, dated numbers, and they carry the argument. The barbell reading in §3 is *interpretation of* those numbers and is labelled as such. No likelihood or severity scores are asserted: assigning 1 to 5 ratings would dress judgement as measurement. Every figure is dated, and a companion `reproduce.py` tabulates and cites each one from `supply_constraints.csv`.

**On verification.** For this edition the event-driven and structural claims were checked against their issuing sources (the IEA, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, PJM, the US Bureau of Reclamation, the European Commission, company filings, and the relevant court dockets) as part of the companion PESTLE scan, v1.0 (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21466083). Market-live readings (prices, spreads, share prices) are 21 July 2026 readings and will move. Where two sources give different values for the same quantity, both are shown rather than averaged.

## 2. The six factors (the data)

**① Capital spend running ahead of revenue, and the credit channel underneath it.** Combined 2026 capital guidance across the big-four hyperscalers sums to approximately $710B on the Q1-2026 guides (Microsoft approximately $190B; Alphabet $195–205B, raised on 22 July 2026 from $180–190B, itself up from $175–185B; Amazon approximately $200B; Meta $125–145B, raised from $115–135B), and approximately $750B across the top five. Reported aggregates track somewhat below that, at $650–700B, so the number is better read as a range than as a point. Accelerators carry a roughly 2 to 3-year refresh clock, so unmonetised clusters face write-downs on a 12 to 36-month horizon.

**Two qualifications belong here, and both cut against the starkest reading of this factor.**

First, part of the increase is price rather than capacity. Microsoft attributes roughly $25B of its approximately $190B to higher component pricing, and Meta's raise cites higher component pricing, especially memory, alongside added data-centre costs. A guidance raise driven by the chokepoint in ④ is cost inflation passing through the capex line, not additional conviction about demand, and the two should not be read as the same signal.

Second, the revenue side of the comparison is the weakest number in this register. Incremental annual AI revenue has been carried at $20–30B, an analyst estimate, and 2026 company disclosures suggest that understates it: Microsoft reported an AI business at a $37B annualised run rate, up 123% year on year, and Google Cloud grew 63% year on year to $20.02B in Q1 2026, with Alphabet's chief executive describing the company as compute constrained in the near term. Those are company-reported figures defined differently from an industry increment, so they do not simply replace it, but the gap between capital and revenue is probably narrower than a $710B against $20–30B framing implies. The comparison remains directional rather than a precise ratio: total hyperscaler capex, not all of it AI-specific, set against an AI-only revenue increment.

Three additions sharpen this factor in 2026. First, scale off balance sheet: Moody's reports approximately $662B of data-centre lease commitments signed but not commenced, sitting off balance sheet under GAAP and larger than the same firms' combined on-balance-sheet debt. Second, the debt itself has been quantified: JPMorgan projects roughly $4.1T of AI-related debt financing through 2030, within an estimated $5.5T of total AI capital spending, and notes that AI-related debt already accounts for about 15% of the corporate-bond universe, with more than $300B issued in 2026 to date. Third, and most consequential for a supply-side reading, this debt is unusually rate-sensitive: it is long-duration paper against long-payback infrastructure, so the Warsh Fed's higher-for-longer stance (held at 3.50–3.75% on 17 June 2026, with the 2026 cut dropped and a median end-2026 rate of 3.8%) raises the carrying cost faster than a token-price decline would. Rates may therefore be the macro lever most likely to turn the build-out's leverage toxic, ahead of any demand disappointment.

The mid-2026 correction sorted the complex by financing structure rather than by sector. Through the same sell-off, Meta held (approximately $648, self-funded capex, up around 10% over four weeks); Micron de-rated on what appears to be real demand (approximately $883, around 30% off its $1,255 intraday high, though still up sharply year-to-date: readings of roughly 200% and 240% both circulate depending on the print date); and Oracle fell hardest (approximately $121, roughly 60% off its 52-week high) after S&P cut it to BBB- on 9 July 2026 citing customer concentration, with OpenAI accounting for about half of Oracle's approximately $638B contract backlog. Cash generation is compressing even where demand is not in question: Amazon's trailing-twelve-month free cash flow fell to approximately $1.2B, a decline of about 95% year on year, which is what a capital cycle of this size does to a balance sheet regardless of how good the underlying business is. The gradient runs from self-funded, to cyclical but real, to debt-financed against a single counterparty. Single-counterparty concentration is the new sub-factor, and Oracle is its worked example.

**Oracle may be better read as a symptom than as an anomaly, though.** The financing in this build-out is substantially circular: labs buy cloud capacity from hyperscalers, hyperscalers and chip vendors hold equity in the labs, chip vendors invest in the deployers who then buy their chips, and hyperscalers prepay memory makers years ahead of delivery. Concentration risk inside a structure like that is not confined to the counterparty where it currently happens to be visible. A single renegotiation propagates through the same debt stack rather than stopping at one balance sheet, which is what makes the $4.1T figure above a systemic number rather than a sum of independent exposures. Oracle is where the exposure surfaced first because its counterparty is named and its rating is public, not because it is the only place it sits.

The reallocation has begun to land in reported earnings. IBM fell 25.21% on 14 July 2026, its worst single day on record, erasing about $68.8B, after pre-announcing a quarter in which clients pulled memory and hardware spend forward and funded it by deferring software, mainframe, and consulting outlays. That is a crowd-out channel: the capital going into the chokepoint described in ④ is partly coming out of adjacent IT budgets rather than out of new money.

Two deflations belong with this factor. Investment-grade credit spreads were approximately 74 bps in June 2026, near multi-decade lows, and high-yield around 2.75%; through the June selloff, investment-grade funds drew approximately $14B of inflows in a single week. The stress since has been issuer-specific rather than a broad widening. And market tolerance is a separate question from direction: guidance is still being raised, so the trigger in §4 has not tripped on the letter, even as the market's reaction to the raises appears to be turning.

**② The binding constraint has moved from chips to the grid, and the price signal is now capped.** Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects US data-centre demand rising from 176 TWh in 2023 (approximately 4.4% of US electricity) to 325–580 TWh (6.7–12%) by 2028. The IEA's data annex puts global data-centre electricity at approximately 416 TWh in 2024, reaching approximately 946 TWh by 2030 in its Base Case, within a scenario range of 669 TWh (Headwinds) to 1,264 TWh (Lift-Off); fleet power-usage effectiveness improves over the period (1.41 in 2024 to approximately 1.29 projected). An AI compute rack draws 30 to 100+ kW against 7 to 10 kW for a conventional rack.

Across PJM's 2026/27 and 2027/28 capacity auctions, data-centre load accounted for approximately $13.8B of capacity cost ($7.3B then $6.5B; PJM's independent market monitor, Monitoring Analytics), and 28–31% of projects drop out at the first interconnection decision even under fast-track rules. The 2026/27 auction cleared at $329.17/MW-day against $28.92 for 2024/25.

**The 2028/29 auction (cleared 14 July 2026) marks a framing shift that v3 records explicitly.** It settled at the $325/MW-day cap, about 2.5% below the prior cap, held there by a price collar agreed with the region's states and FERC; PJM estimates that without the collar it would have cleared at $554.72 and cost about $30B rather than $16.4B. It still cleared 6,831 MW short, with data centres named the primary demand driver and only 525 MW of new generation clearing. With the price capped by design, the constraint now surfaces as shortfall volume rather than as runaway price. Anyone watching the clearing price alone will read a tight market as a calm one.

Equipment sets the hard ceiling underneath all of this. Large power transformers run 128-week lead times, generator step-up units 144 weeks, and extra-high-voltage units 36 to 48 months, up to approximately 60 months in extreme cases (Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025 survey, corroborated by the US Department of Energy and the trade press), all constrained by grain-oriented electrical steel. Where high-voltage substation lead times run 3 to 5 years, developers increasingly turn to on-site generation to bypass the queue, which carries a local externality of its own: xAI's Colossus 2 near Memphis was found operating 59 unpermitted gas turbines, and the federal air-permit loophole that had allowed this was closed in January 2026. Power deployment has a physical speed limit that capital cannot shortcut. Alphabet put first-party words to the shortage on 22 July 2026, describing itself as "still in a supply-constrained environment" with demand outpacing three years of rising investment, and said it would expand its use of third-party cloud capacity in the third quarter as a bridge while it builds its own. A hyperscaler renting capacity because it cannot build fast enough is a shortage signal, and it is worth holding against the over-capacity reading examined under ⑤.

**One qualification on using PJM as the gauge.** PJM is a single US interconnection with its own price collar and its own state politics, so it is a partial instrument rather than a national one. It is also, increasingly, measuring only the load that stayed in the queue: behind-the-meter generation, direct power-purchase agreements, and off-grid energy islands take deployment outside the clearing mechanism altogether. The shortfall should therefore be read as a floor under the constraint rather than as its full extent. And the risk that bypass avoids does not disappear, it changes venue: it reappears as the air-permit, water, and local-litigation exposure described above, where the binding authority is a county board or a state regulator rather than a capacity auction.

Two corrections attach here. Per-query energy has been revised downward, not upward: the widely-cited approximately 2.9 Wh figure has given way to a median Gemini text prompt at approximately 0.24 Wh (Google) and approximately 0.34 Wh (OpenAI) for 2025, though heavier reasoning and multimodal queries run far higher, with some responses at approximately 18–40 Wh. Because usage is shifting toward agentic, multi-step reasoning, the median-text figure understates the per-task trajectory rather than capturing it: efficiency per query and energy per task may move in opposite directions. And the projection literature is itself wide. The IEA 4E EDNA review finds 2030 data-centre-energy projections spanning approximately 200 TWh to approximately 8,000 TWh across 100+ studies, a roughly 40-fold range, triangulates 2023 at approximately 300–380 TWh, attributes the widest figures to lower-quality temporal extrapolation, and documents two prior energy-demand panics that did not materialise. That caveat applies to every figure in this factor.

**A correction to carry forward, since a prior reading has been superseded.** The US Bureau of Reclamation's early-2026 studies projected Lake Powell breaching the 3,490-ft minimum power pool at Glen Canyon Dam as early as August to December 2026. Emergency operations have deferred that: Reclamation cut the water-year 2026 release from 7.48 to 6.00 million acre-feet and drew on Flaming Gorge, and the May 2026 study keeps Powell above 3,504 ft through year-end in all three inflow scenarios. The August 2026 breach should not be repeated as a live expectation. It is deferred rather than resolved, with inflows still historically dry at about 31% of average, and it remains the clearest instance of water and electricity binding through a single body. A US heatwave in early July 2026 showed the compound version, where high ambient temperatures raise evaporative-cooling water demand at the same moment peak air-conditioning load stresses the grid.

Energy price shocks feed back into ①. A mid-July 2026 stacking of a Hormuz re-escalation and Russia's full diesel-export ban pushed Brent to about $88, with European diesel sharply higher. The EIA's base case remains a return toward approximately $70 by Q4 2026 on OPEC+ spare capacity and a clearing tanker backlog, so on current evidence this reads as a tail risk to AI operating cost rather than the central case.

**③ Leading-edge fabrication is a single geography.** TSMC produces approximately 90% of leading-edge logic, and CoWoS advanced packaging is effectively all Taiwan-based with no second source. Diversification still leaves leading-edge production Taiwan-bound through roughly 2028 to 2030. The capability cannot be seized by force: the lithography tools are reportedly capable of remote disablement and depend on imported inputs, so a Strait disruption would mean a global advanced-compute freeze rather than a transfer of capacity. This factor is unchanged from v2.2, and it remains the convergence event, striking chips, a shipping lane, and the financial system at once.

**④ Manufactured and licensed single-points-of-failure, and the chokepoint set has widened.** Beyond logic, the chain narrows to a few suppliers.

*Memory.* An HBM and DRAM shortage is reported to run to approximately 2028. Nanya, a commodity-DRAM maker and therefore a read on the DRAM leg rather than on HBM specifically, posted revenue up 583% year-on-year in Q1 2026, off a depressed base and driven by a greater-than-70% DRAM price spike rather than by added volume. One structural change may cut against the historic boom-bust read: memory supply contracts have reportedly lengthened to 3 to 5 years with prepayments of up to 30%, a de-cyclicalisation of a trade that has always mean-reverted. If that holds, the shortage is less likely to end the way previous memory shortages ended.

*Packaging, and a second chokepoint.* CoWoS remains the primary advanced-packaging bottleneck. A second has now been named alongside it: ASE Technology, the world's largest outsourced assembly-and-test firm, which raised 2026 capex to $8.5B and is identified as the critical node for co-packaged optics. This is a node distinct from TSMC's CoWoS, which means the packaging constraint is not a single point that a single mitigation addresses.

*Optics.* EUV lithography optics remain sole-sourced through ASML and Zeiss.

*Materials, licensed.* China refines most of the world's rare earths and dominates heavy-rare-earth separation and magnet-making; earlier editions of this paper gave approximately 91% of refining and approximately 60% of magnet-grade mining (IEA, with Rhodium Group). The bottleneck is processing rather than mining, which is why the main Western alternatives (MP Materials, USA Rare Earth) are not expected at full magnet production until 2027, and why new mines do not resolve it. Beijing has used the position as an export-control lever repeatedly: gallium and germanium from 2023, a US-specific ban on gallium, germanium and antimony in December 2024, and an April 2025 per-shipment licensing regime over seven medium and heavy rare earths and the magnets containing them, which pushed EU heavy-rare-earth prices (dysprosium, terbium) to roughly six times China's domestic level (IEA). An October 2025 expansion reached extraterritorially, so that any foreign product containing 0.1% or more Chinese-origin rare earths would have required a licence, before being suspended for a year under a US-China truce. That suspension is reviewed around November 2026, which is the hard planning horizon in this register. (Earlier versions gave 27 November 2026 as a precise date; the verification pass did not confirm the day, so v3 states the month.)

*Materials, physical.* The 28 February 2026 strike on Qatar's Ras Laffan took an estimated 30% of global semiconductor-grade helium offline, with spot prices up 40–100%. Helium is co-produced with LNG and stops when the plant does, and recovery is slow: QatarEnergy ties restart to a ceasefire, and LNG repairs are estimated at up to five years given a global turbine shortage. The deflation is real, though: TSMC reported no notable impact and SK Hynix said it had diversified and secured inventory, so the shock registered in storage (helium-sealed drives reported sold out through 2026) rather than in leading-edge output. These two should not be flattened into one risk, and an earlier draft of this section did exactly that. They throttle the same chain, but only one of them can be waited out. Helium behaved as an elastic buffer problem: fabs held inventory, TSMC reported no notable impact, and the shock surfaced in storage rather than in leading-edge output. Heavy-rare-earth separation is a structural capital-equipment and permitting bottleneck, where the limit is separation chemistry and environmental consent rather than stock on hand, and where no amount of buffer brings Western magnet capacity forward of 2027. A physical shock to a buffered input is a months-long disruption; a licensing monopoly over a process no one else can run at scale is a multi-year one. The licensed leg is the more severe of the two, and it is the leg that belongs in the tail described in §3.

Inventory exhaustion at any one of these nodes stalls downstream assembly.

**⑤ Commoditisation from below.** Open models compress the frontier premium, with reported effects on margins rather than a documented collapse. Moonshot's Kimi K3 (16 July 2026) is the current datapoint: a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model priced at $3/$15 per million tokens, scoring near the top closed tier on one aggregate index while trailing the leading closed model overall, and reported by Axios to have beaten both Anthropic's Fable 5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol in blind front-end coding tests. [1] A price index of frontier tokens (Citadel's read) fell approximately 20% from its May 2026 peak as usage substituted toward cheaper models. Chinese industrial policy is now explicitly behind open weights, with Xi framing open-weight models as a global public good at the July 2026 World AI Conference. The supply-side relevance is that a cheaper substitute lowers the revenue that has to service the capital in ①, without lowering the capital.

**A caveat on how this factor is measured, since it is the least quantified of the six.** The lens stated in §1 is yield on a deployed megawatt, and that quantity is not directly observable: firms do not report revenue per megawatt of deployed capacity, and a token price index is a stand-in for it rather than a measure of it. What can be observed is the direction of the two terms separately, with token prices falling while capital cost per megawatt rises on component pricing, per ①. This factor is therefore carried on a directional argument rather than on a measured ratio, and it should be read with that weaker footing in mind until a better proxy exists.

> **Inverse-risk row: the over-capacity tail (monitored, not base case).**
>
> **The claim.** Several large spenders are moving to monetise surplus capacity. xAI has leased its Colossus 1 cluster (approximately 222,000 GPUs, reportedly running near 11% utilisation) to Anthropic (a reported $45B over three years) and to Google (approximately $920M/month); Meta went further on 1 July 2026, when Bloomberg reported it is building Meta Compute, a business to sell access to its surplus AI infrastructure, alongside a reported prospective compute lease to Anthropic worth up to approximately $10B over two years. [1] Bloomberg described the plan as still in development and subject to change. Some analysts read a hyperscaler even considering a net-seller role as early evidence of over-capacity, which would flatten rather than steepen the HBM demand curve. That is the opposite tail to the shortage premise running through ① to ④.
>
> **The market has now priced a first response, and the shape of it matters.** On the report Meta gained about 9%, while the rental specialists fell hard: CoreWeave about 14% in a session and Nebius about 17%, both down roughly 35% over the following month. The mechanism is two-sided rather than demand-side. Meta had contracted approximately $48B from those two firms, roughly $27B with Nebius in March 2026 and $35.2B in total with CoreWeave after an April expansion, so a self-supplying Meta withdraws a large buyer from the rental market at the same moment it adds a seller to it. That is a reallocation of who sells compute. It is not the same event as demand for compute falling, and the two are worth separating carefully because they produce similar-looking price action in the rental names.
>
> **Why this register does not treat it as a base case.** Leasing spare capacity is the ordinary cloud business model, not a confession of overbuild. At least three readings fit the same facts better: capital efficiency (monetising idle hours), burn mitigation, or demand *strength*, since it was reportedly Anthropic, short of compute, that proposed the arrangements, which would make the deal a shortage signal rather than a glut signal. The plan is described as still in development. Two further constraints sit on the strong version of the claim. Meta's CoreWeave contract reportedly contains a no-sublease provision barring resale of capacity it rents, which caps how much of any Meta offering could be resold rather than self-supplied. And several observers make the opposite inference from the same fact: a firm with meaningful surplus to sell implies a market large enough to absorb it, which reads as growth rather than as glut. Set against the claim is a set of primary shortage indicators pointing the other way: memory reportedly sold out for 2026 and contracted 3 to 5 years ahead with prepayments; a capacity auction clearing 6,831 MW short; and Alphabet, on 22 July 2026, calling itself supply-constrained and turning to third-party capacity as a bridge, which is a hyperscaler renting out of shortage rather than selling out of surplus.
>
> **Why it is not dismissed either.** A hyperscaler selling compute is a genuine first, and the capex-to-revenue gap in ① and the depreciation lag behind it are both real. The honest position is that this is one reading of one firm's launched rental business and a set of leases, each of which has an interpretation other than over-capacity available, which is a reason to monitor it rather than to re-shape the assessment around it.
>
> **What would upgrade it from tail to base case** (any of): multiple hyperscalers turning net sellers; published idle-fleet utilisation data confirming sustained low utilisation; HBM contracts renegotiated downward; or actual capex-guidance cuts, as distinct from slower raises. None of these was present as of 21 July 2026.

**⑥ The export-control two-stack, now reaching model access.** Earlier versions treated export controls as a cost amplifier. That understated them after June 2026.

On 12 June 2026 the Commerce Department used an "Is-Informed" letter, a BIS instrument that requires no rulemaking, to require an export licence for any foreign person to access Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5. [1] This was the first application of export controls to an AI model rather than to the chips that train it. Compliance was infeasible at short notice, so the models went dark worldwide before a trusted-partner carve-out on 26 June and a full withdrawal on 30 June; Fable was restored on 1 July and Mythos more gradually. The episode did not stay contained to one firm: within days OpenAI limited the release of GPT-5.6 at the administration's request. Two features may make this durable rather than a one-off. The authority can be applied to any model BIS judges to present a military-intelligence risk, without new legislation. And, as President Macron warned at the G7, a switch that can be thrown from one day to the next is itself a reason for allies not to buy US models, which is a spur to sovereign, locally-hosted open-weight procurement across the EU, the Gulf, and Japan. That is a supply constraint that generates its own substitution, and it feeds ⑤.

The hardware two-stack continues alongside it. The US approved H200 sales to China in December 2025 under a 25% export tax codified in January 2026, but by March 2026 the trade had stalled at both ends, with US shipments routed through a domestic security screening that triggers the tariff while China restricted imports to push its firms toward domestic parts. The policy direction now runs in the other direction too: Axios reported on 20 July 2026 that the administration is reviving plans to restrict Chinese AI models following Kimi K3, weighing Entity List designations for Chinese AI labs, federal procurement restrictions, security advisories, and liability requirements, deliberately short of an outright ban. It is contested internally, with AI adviser David Sacks warning that leading closed-source labs are seeking to use government power against open-source competitors. Two deflations apply: this is reported deliberation rather than an issued rule, and downloadable open weights may make any such restriction difficult to enforce.

**One limit on this instrument, and it cuts both ways.** Model-access control is soft enforcement set against a physical chokepoint. Weights leak, API traffic can be proxied through neutral jurisdictions, and local fine-tuning sits outside a hosted endpoint entirely, so ⑥ should not be read as carrying the enforceability of ③. What the June episode shows is narrower and more useful than a general claim about state power: the instrument bit because Fable and Mythos are closed-weight hosted models, which is precisely the case it can reach. It is the open-weight case it cannot. That asymmetry is the point at which ⑥ feeds ⑤, since a control that works on hosted frontier models and fails on downloadable ones operates, in effect, as a subsidy to the substitutes.

The governance layer is bifurcating on the same line. At the July 2026 World AI Conference in Shanghai, China launched the World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO), a governance body with 29 founding states, positioned against the US-led Pax Silica supply-chain grouping (launched December 2025 with about two dozen members and reported at 35 by its second summit, including India, the UK, and the EU). Kazakhstan is in both. The effect is a governance-and-supply order splitting along the same seam as the hardware stack rather than cutting across it.

Two further constraints belong here rather than in §5, because they gate availability rather than price it. The state is becoming a prospective owner as well as a gatekeeper: OpenAI proposed (Financial Times, 2 July 2026) donating 5% of its equity, roughly $42.6B on its $852B March mark, into a US public wealth fund, with an established federal precedent in the 10% government stake in Intel after an $8.9B CHIPS investment. It remains conceptual, Anthropic has stated it is not in talks, and its practical value is easily overstated, since a passive, illiquid minority stake in a capped-profit structure facing large capex calls is closer to a political token than a conventional ownership position. The point worth holding is narrower: the same state is positioned both to gate a frontier model and to own a share of its maker. And standards are becoming the real rate-limiter on regulation: EU AI Act enforcement over general-purpose models takes effect on 2 August 2026, with fines up to 3% of global turnover or an order to restrict or withdraw a model, while the Annex III high-risk obligations have been decoupled from the calendar under the Digital Omnibus and linked instead to the availability of harmonised CEN-CENELEC standards, a delay of up to roughly sixteen months plus a six-month transition. Where the standards lag, enforcement lags with them. The near-term effect in Europe may be less a reprieve than a freeze, since deployers cannot certify against standards that do not yet exist.

## 3. The barbell (interpretation)

The six factors do not describe a smooth risk gradient. They describe a barbell. At one end sits a high-probability base case: volatile, energy-constrained growth with sharp capital corrections (① and ②). At the other sits a low-probability, uncapped-severity tail (③ Taiwan, and the licensed-materials leg of ④). The middle is thin.

**The practical implication.** The compute layer appears resilient to ordinary competitive shocks but fragile to a single macro-systemic one, and standard expected-value thinking *understates* the tail precisely because its probability is low while its severity is unbounded. The shape, not the average, is the point.

**The stated asymmetry, tested and retained.** The register's characterisation, fragile to catastrophe and robust to defection, has been examined against the over-capacity evidence set out under ⑤ and is retained. Commoditisation from below is defection, and the base case absorbs it: cheaper open models compress margins and lower the revenue servicing the capital, which is a correction mechanism rather than a break. The genuinely destabilising version would be the inverse tail, in which demand flattens beneath the build-out rather than outrunning it. On the evidence available at 21 July 2026 that remains one reading of one preliminary transaction, against a set of primary indicators pointing the other way, so it is carried as a named and triggered tail rather than as a softening of the thesis. Should its upgrade conditions be met, the shape does not invert so much as collapse. A glut does not produce a mirrored barbell; it produces a single mode of stranded assets and write-downs, with the tail in ③ and ④ still attached but no longer the thing that matters most. The register would need rebuilding rather than amending, which is why the row is named here rather than left implicit.

**On correlation, which the shape depends on.** The six factors are not independent, and treating them as though they were would understate the tail. ② feeds ① directly, since an energy-price shock raises operating cost and brings write-downs forward. ④ feeds ① twice over, once through component pricing inflating the capex line and once through the crowd-out now visible in adjacent IT budgets. ⑥ feeds ⑤, since a control that reaches hosted models and not downloadable ones operates as a subsidy to the substitutes. And ③ is less a factor than a convergence, striking chips, a shipping lane, and the financial system in a single event, which is why it sits in the tail rather than in the base case. The practical consequence is that the tail is fatter than the individual likelihoods would suggest, because the paths into it are shared rather than separate.

**What the barbell is, and is not.** It is a bimodal scenario model for thinking about capital allocation, not a claim about an underlying physical probability distribution, and it should not be defended as though it were one. Two things would end its usefulness. If the inverse tail materialises, the distribution collapses to the single mode just described. And if the middle fills in, with persistent margin compression and a slow grind in place of either a sharp correction or a catastrophe, the shape simply stops describing the situation. Nothing in the six factors guarantees the middle stays thin. That thinness is an observation about the present configuration, and it is the first thing to re-examine rather than the last.

**Two things v3 has changed in the reading itself.** ② now expresses its constraint partly as volume rather than price, because a policy collar caps the price signal, and a capped price is not the same as a loosening constraint. And ⑥ has moved from an amplifier to a factor, because an instrument that removes models from the market for a period of days is acting on availability rather than on cost.

## 5. Cost amplifiers (policy, not standalone factors)

**US tariffs** raise the landed cost of imported hardware and grid equipment. The average effective US tariff rate was approximately 9.1% on its 2026 reading, the highest since 1946, following the Section 232 and Section 122 measures. The statutory basis is in flux: on 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 (*Learning Resources v. Trump*) that IEEPA does not authorise the President to impose tariffs, striking down the 2025 reciprocal and fentanyl tariffs; Section 232 tariffs were unaffected, and the administration replaced the struck-down duties within hours under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. The effective rate should therefore be treated as a moving figure rather than a settled one.

Tariffs do not change the physical constraints above; they make them more expensive to work around. Export controls were listed here in v2.2 and have been promoted to factor ⑥, for the reason given in §1.
